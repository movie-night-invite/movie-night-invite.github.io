The film

What we're watching

San Francisco. Joe is a musician whose career has politely stopped returning his calls. Angela is at home, and unimpressed. Their marriage now runs entirely on logistics and low-grade resentment — so they do what couples do when the vibes are bad: invite other people over and hope those people fix it.

Upstairs live Piña, a divorced therapist who is professionally unshockable, and Hawk, a widower who has clearly made peace with several things Joe and Angela have not. They come down. They are charming. Then they mention, very casually, how they actually live. The salad hasn't even arrived yet.

One apartment, one very long evening, and the most honest conversation this couple has had in about a decade — most of it conducted while holding a wine glass they would love to put down and absolutely cannot.

Directed by Olivia Wilde. Written by Will McCormack and Rashida Jones, from Cesc Gay's Spanish film The People Upstairs. With Seth Rogen (Joe), Olivia Wilde (Angela), Penélope Cruz (Piña) and Edward Norton (Hawk). Cinematography by Adam Newport-Berra, score by Devonté Hynes. Premiered at Sundance in January 2026; released by A24. 107 minutes.

The case for it

Why this one

  • One room, four people, nowhere to hide. Nobody loses the plot walking to the kitchen. Worst case you miss a joke, and someone will replay it for you, badly.
  • It picks a fight worth having. Honesty or peace — which one actually keeps a long relationship alive? The film flatly refuses to settle it. Convenient, since that's what 9:30 is for.
  • The reviews are getting embarrassing. 97% on Rotten Tomatoes from 272 critics, 82 on Metacritic, and several of them reaching for Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? about a movie starring Seth Rogen.
  • Everyone understood the assignment. Norton is having the best time he's had in a decade, Cruz is comfortably the most dangerous person at the table, and Rogen quietly does real acting — which we are all going to be normal about.
  • It respects a weeknight. 107 minutes. Out by half ten, emotionally a little worse, socially much better.

While it plays

Things worth catching

  • The floor plan. Wilde stages the flat like a play: who sits, who stands, who suddenly needs something from the kitchen. It is never really about the kitchen.
  • When the camera finally blinks. Long, patient takes. Notice the moment the film loses its nerve and cuts — and whose face it cuts to. It's usually someone who'd rather not be there.
  • Where the music isn't. Devonté Hynes' score turns up rarely. The silences are chosen just as carefully, and they're worse.
  • Who's actually hosting. Piña is a therapist. Count how often the guests are running the evening while the hosts are merely catering it.
  • The glasses and the plates. How wrecked the table is tells you precisely how deep into the night you are. It's the film's clock, and it runs fast.
  • The pivot. There's one line where the laugh curdles. See whether the whole room finds the same one — and if you're alone on it, that's information.
  • Live editing. Rogen and Wilde play two people rewriting their sentences mid-air. Watch what gets said against what gets visibly swallowed.

Afterwards

Questions, served in courses

Numbered so we can jump around. Someone will want to go straight to number 9.

First courseWhile everyone is still being polite

  1. Whose side were you on when the neighbours knocked — and had that moved by the time they left?
  2. Which of the four would you genuinely want living above you? Show your working.
  3. Was this ever actually a dinner, or was it an ambush with side dishes?

MainThe part we came for

  1. Piña offers a binary: separate, or rebuild the marriage into something else entirely. Real choice, or a therapist doing a party trick?
  2. Staying together for the kids — generous, cowardly, or simply less effort than moving out? Does the film think it knows?
  3. Hawk and Piña present their arrangement as the more honest life. Are they honest, or just better rehearsed?
  4. Critics keep reaching for Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? Is this film as cruel, or does it let everybody off with a warning?
  5. Does the comedy protect the characters, or protect us? Be honest: you laughed at the worst bit.
  6. Who told the biggest lie tonight — and was it to someone else, or to themselves?

DessertBefore anyone finds their coat

  1. Did the ending earn how it left them? And which ending did you want, before you got the one you got?
  2. The same night, told from upstairs. What's that film?
  3. One sentence to Joe. One sentence to Angela. You get no follow-up.
